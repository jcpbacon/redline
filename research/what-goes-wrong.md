## Detail per clause

**1. Auto-renewal / negative-option / evergreen clauses.** Plain-language: the contract keeps renewing and charging you unless you take an affirmative, often-hard-to-find action to cancel before a deadline. Appears in: consumer ToS/subscriptions and SMB vendor/software contracts (annual auto-renew with 60/90-day opt-out windows). Why readers miss it: the renewal clause is usually a single sentence deep in a "Term and Termination" section, and cancellation mechanics (which channel, which deadline) are described elsewhere or in a separate cancellation flow, so the two pieces of information a reader needs are never shown together. The FTC's own complaint trend (rising complaint rate year over year) and the scale of a single 2024 settlement (~500,000 consumers) both point to this being a high-frequency, high-volume harm.

**2. Mandatory arbitration + class-action waiver.** Plain-language: you give up the right to sue in court or join a class action; disputes go to a private arbitrator instead. Appears in: ToS, freelance platform agreements, consumer financial contracts. Why readers miss it: it's boilerplate near the end of the document, phrased in procedural/venue language ("governing law and dispute resolution") that doesn't sound like it's taking away a right — the CFPB's finding that 3 in 4 consumers didn't even know they were covered is the direct evidence of this "buried and misunderstood" pattern.

**3. Non-compete clauses.** Plain-language: after the engagement ends, you can't work for a competitor or start a competing business for some period/geography. Appears in: freelance/independent-contractor agreements and SMB vendor or employment-adjacent contracts. Why readers miss it: often framed as standard "protective" boilerplate near confidentiality clauses, and independent contractors in particular often don't expect employee-style restrictions to apply to them. The FTC comment volume (>26,000, dominated by support for a ban) and the Small Business Majority hiring-impact numbers are direct evidence this actively blocks people's livelihoods, not just a theoretical risk.

**4. Payment terms / late payment exposure.** Plain-language: the contract sets (or fails to set) when and how you get paid, with no real teeth (interest, late fees, kill fees) if the other side pays late or not at all. Appears in: freelance/independent-contractor agreements. Why readers miss it: payment terms read as administrative rather than risky, so freelancers focus scrutiny on scope/IP/liability clauses and skip past net-30/45/60 language or the absence of a late-fee clause — even though this is where survey data shows the most people actually lose money (50%+ report payment trouble, avg. $6,000/year lost).

**5. Liability caps & indemnification carve-outs.** Plain-language: a cap limits how much one party can be forced to pay the other if something goes wrong, and indemnification clauses can shift open-ended legal costs onto one party — carve-outs from the cap (e.g., for indemnification, confidentiality, data breaches) can make that exposure effectively unlimited. Appears in: SMB vendor contracts. Why readers miss it: the interaction between the liability cap section and the (often far-separated) indemnification section is the actual risk, and few non-lawyers read the two together; the market data shows carve-outs are the norm, not the exception, in negotiated deals.

**6. Security deposit / repair-liability clauses.** Plain-language: sets what the landlord can deduct from a deposit and the process/timeline for returning it. Appears in: residential leases. Why readers miss it: often relegated to an addendum or referenced by statute citation rather than spelled out, and tenants don't reread it until move-out, by which point negotiating leverage is gone. NY AG complaint volume (~5,000 since 2023) is concrete evidence, though the research did not find a comparable multi-state complaint count.

**7. Unilateral amendment clauses.** Plain-language: "we may change these terms at any time," often with continued use as acceptance. Appears in: ToS. Why readers miss it: it's presented as routine housekeeping language, and the actual mechanism of harm (a later, more one-sided term slipped in without direct notice) only becomes visible in hindsight, and only if the reader happens to re-check the terms. Case law shows courts are skeptical of enforcing changes made this way, especially for arbitration additions.

**8. Rental "junk fees" / hidden mandatory fees.** Plain-language: mandatory fees (admin, amenity, "convenience," processing) disclosed late or bundled in a way that hides the true total cost. Appears in: residential leases and, in adjacent form, SMB vendor contracts with hidden surcharges. Why readers miss it: individual fee line items look small and are often described euphemistically; the harm is cumulative and only obvious when totaled against the advertised price. FTC enforcement scale (~500,000 consumers in one settlement) is the strongest signal here, though this is more a fee-disclosure practice than a single identifiable "clause."

## What I could not find

- **IP assignment / work-for-hire overreach (freelance agreements):** Multiple legal/advisory sources describe this as a real and costly risk (one sourced example: a $15,000 legal-fee dispute over ownership of a freelancer's proprietary tooling, https://patentpc.com/blog/how-to-prevent-ip-ownership-disputes-with-contractors-and-freelancers), but no complaint-volume, survey-percentage, or enforcement-action data was found within the search budget to rank this against the others. Dropped from the ranked table per the "no unsourced volume claims" guardrail; flagged here as a plausible high-severity candidate worth a follow-up search pass.
- **Personal guarantees (SMB vendor/lease contracts):** Legal explainer sources confirm the mechanism (lenders/landlords can sue guarantors personally, garnish wages, force asset sales) but none of the sources surfaced in this search budget had complaint counts, survey data, or case-frequency statistics.
- **Non-solicitation clauses:** Similar to non-competes in mechanism, but no volume or frequency data was found distinguishing non-solicits from non-competes empirically; existing sources only describe enforcement mechanics, not how often disputes occur.
- **Confidentiality/NDA overreach and exclusivity clauses:** Not searched in this pass at all given the 12-search cap; genuinely unaddressed, not just thin.
- **Fee escalators specifically (as distinct from general junk fees):** Not found as a separately quantified category; rolled into the rental junk-fees finding above but not confirmed as its own high-frequency complaint driver.
- A primary-source (FTC/CFPB own page, not a law-firm summary) citation for the auto-renewal complaint-rate trend (42/day 2021 to 70+/day 2024) could not be directly fetched — the FTC press release returned HTTP 403 to the fetch tool. The figure is corroborated by a law-firm advisory (Jones Day) that is presumably drawing on FTC's own docket/complaint data, but this is a secondary citation, not a primary one.
